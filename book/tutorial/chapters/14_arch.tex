\section{The complete transceiver}\label{sec:arch-block}

\Cref{fig:arch} puts the whole chip on one page. It is a single-lane transceiver: one transmitter, one receiver and one clock generator on the same \sgx{} die, connected to each other through the off-chip REF channel (\cref{sec:refchannel}) or through an on-chip loopback.

\paragraph{Transmit path} A 16-bit word arrives every 3.2\,ns (312.5\,MHz), from the user or from the on-chip PRBS7/31 generator (\cref{sec:prbs}). CMOS on 1.2\,V performs the first 16:4 serialization; HBT CML on the 2.5\,V analog supply performs the 4:2 and the final half-rate 2:1, built three times so the driver receives $D_{n+1}$, $D_n$ and $D_{n-1}$ for its three FFE taps (\cref{sec:serializer,sec:ffe-impl}). The CML driver launches 1\,Vppd into \qty{50}{\ohm} per side (\cref{sec:cml-driver}).

\paragraph{Receive path} Behind the ESD diodes and the \qty{100}{\ohm} termination (\cref{sec:esd-pad}), a loopback multiplexer selects the pad or the near-end loopback. The CTLE adds 0--12\,dB of peaking (\cref{ch:ctle}), the DFE summer subtracts $w_1$ and $w_2$ times the previous decisions (\cref{sec:dfe-timing}), and four samplers decide: data D0, D1 and edges E0, E1 (\cref{sec:sampler}). The deserializer delivers 16 data and 16 edge bits at 312.5\,MHz (\cref{sec:deserializer}); the PRBS checker counts errors.

\paragraph{Clocking and control} An integer-N PLL with an LC VCO multiplies the 50\,MHz reference by $N=50$ to 2.5\,GHz (\cref{ch:pll}). The TX gets it through buffers and $\div2$ dividers; the RX gets I and Q from a polyphase filter (\cref{sec:quadrature}), rotated by two phase interpolators (PIs) under the digital CDR loop filter (\cref{sec:picdr}). An SPI port writes every setting.

Notice three things. The 2.5\,GHz PLL clock is used twice: divided down for the TX (fixed phase), and split into I/Q and rotated by the PIs for the RX (recovered phase). \end{figure}

\subsection{The clocking plan}\label{sec:ch14-clock}

\Cref{fig:ch14-clock} follows each clock from the reference to the last flip-flop. All of the frequencies come from one relation between the bit rate, the half-rate clock and the word rate.

\begin{keyeq}[The \OS{} clock frequencies]
\begin{equation}
  f_\mathrm{clk} = \frac{\fb}{2} = N f_\mathrm{ref} = \qty{2.5}{\giga\hertz},\qquad
  f_\mathrm{word} = \frac{\fb}{16} = \qty{312.5}{\mega\hertz},\qquad
  \Delta t_\mathrm{PI} = \frac{1}{64\,f_\mathrm{clk}} = \qty{6.25}{\pico\second}
  \label{eq:ch14-clk}
\end{equation}
\end{keyeq}

The TX needs one clock phase: the final 2:1 mux uses both edges of the 2.5\,GHz clock as its select (\cref{sec:serializer}). The RX needs four phases half a UI apart: data samplers on the edges of I (eye centers), edge samplers on the edges of Q (crossings), all moved together by the CDR in 6.25\,ps steps. The RX dividers run from the \emph{recovered} clock, so the deserializer and the CDR logic sit in a clock domain that is not phase-locked to the TX word clock; a small FIFO crosses back to the core (\cref{sec:deserializer}). In near-end loopback both ends share the PLL, so the frequency offset is zero. Through a board to a second chip, the CDR must absorb up to $\pm300$\,ppm (\cref{sec:picdr}). \Cref{sec:ch14-10g} keeps this plan for a 10\,Gb/s target with the clocks doubled, and takes I and Q from a $\div2$ instead of the polyphase filter.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\input{tutorial/tikz/ch14_clock}
\caption{\textbf{One 2.5\,GHz PLL serves the whole chip; the TX divides it, the RX rotates it.} Top: the frequency at every node. The TX chain divides by 2, 4 and 8 down to the 312.5\,MHz word clock. The RX chain first makes four phases with the polyphase filter, rotates them with the two PIs, and divides the recovered clock for the deserializer and for the CDR loop, which updates every 8\,UI at 625\,MHz. Bottom: what the four phases do. D0 and D1 sample the eye centers on the two edges of I; E0 and E1 sample the crossings on the edges of Q. One 400\,ps clock period carries two bits and contains 64 PI steps.}
\label{fig:ch14-clock}
\end{figure}

\begin{insight}
Three boundaries organize the chip, and most integration bugs live on them: the \textbf{speed boundary} between CMOS and CML (1.25\,Gb/s in the TX, 2.5\,Gb/s in the RX), which needs level converters and half a slow-clock cycle of timing margin; the \textbf{supply boundary} between 1.2 and 2.5\,V, where every $V_\mathrm{CE}$ must stay below $\mathrm{BV}_\mathrm{CEO}$; and the \textbf{clock-domain boundary} between the TX clock and the recovered RX clock. Draw all three on the floorplan first.
\end{insight}

\paragraph{The link budget in one paragraph} Both budgets close (\cref{fig:ch14-budget}). In voltage, REF with only the 2-tap TX de-emphasis leaves a 131\,mV statistical eye at $10^{-12}$; noise ($2Q\sign=21$\,mV), jitter (3.5\,mV), offset (10\,mV) and sensitivity (20\,mV) leave a \textbf{76\,mV} margin by linear addition, 89\,mV statistically (\cref{sec:noisebudget}), and the full partition ($-6$\,dB preset + 2-tap DFE, 172\,mV noise-free PRBS7 eye, \cref{sec:eq-partition}) only adds to it. In time, 40\,ps of DJ plus $2Q\times1.32$\,ps of RJ give TJ = \textbf{58.6\,ps = 0.293\,UI}, just inside 0.3\,UI (\cref{sec:jitter-budget}). Timing is the tighter budget, and its largest line is the CDR's own dither.

\section{Design for 10, guarantee 5}\label{sec:ch14-10g}

Everything so far describes the 5\,Gb/s baseline that the rest of this book derives. Can the same architecture go faster? The transistors are not what stops it: a 100\,ps UI still holds about fifty \sgx{} CML gate delays (\cref{fig:ch1-speed}), while 130\,nm CMOS ran out of margin at 5\,Gb/s. The limits are the channel, the clocks and the timing budget. This section shows that 10\,Gb/s NRZ fits the same architecture with six changes, and sets the project target accordingly: \textbf{design every high-speed block for 10\,Gb/s, and keep 5\,Gb/s over REF as the pass criterion of first silicon.} The 10\,Gb/s column of \cref{tab:specs} collects the resulting numbers.

\paragraph{The equalizer sees decibels, not bits per second} \Cref{fig:ch14-rate}(a) repeats the partition study of \cref{sec:eq-partition} at 5, 8 and 10\,Gb/s NRZ and at 10\,Gb/s PAM4, with the CTLE poles moved to each Nyquist frequency. Over REF, 10\,Gb/s NRZ meets $-30.1$\,dB at 5\,GHz and the worst-case eye closes to 18\,mV. Over half the line, 0.5\,m with the same pads, it opens to \textbf{193\,mV}, more than the 134\,mV of the 5\,Gb/s baseline over REF. We call this channel \textbf{REF-10}: $-15.3$\,dB at 5\,GHz. The rule behind it is simple. The equalizer only sees the loss at Nyquist; two links with the same loss need the same equalizer, whatever their bit rates. PAM4 at 10\,Gb/s keeps the 2.5\,GHz Nyquist frequency of REF but gives up two-thirds of each sub-eye: 14\,mV over REF and 97\,mV over REF-10, below NRZ at both lengths and with three slicers per sample (\cref{sec:pam4}).

\paragraph{Six changes} The block diagram of \cref{fig:arch} stays: half-rate clocking, the CMOS/CML split, FFE + CTLE + DFE, a PI-based bang-bang CDR. Six things change.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Clocks at 5\,GHz.} The half-rate clock doubles. A 10\,GHz LC VCO followed by a CML $\div2$ gives I and Q at 5\,GHz, with the quadrature set by the divider rather than by matching (\cref{sec:quadrature}). A second $\div2$ gives the 2.5\,GHz I and Q of the 5\,Gb/s mode, so one register selects the rate and the polyphase filter goes away. The cost: the team's 2.4\,GHz PLL~\cite{smacd2026pll} is reused for its loop and its flow, not for its tank.
  \item \textbf{The timing budget.} Jitter set in picoseconds does not shrink with the UI. \Cref{we:ch14-10g} shows that the budget closes only if those terms roughly halve; this is the main extra work.
  \item \textbf{One more CML stage on each side.} CMOS stays at 1.25\,Gb/s per lane or less (\cref{sec:serializer}). The TX becomes CMOS 16:8 on a 625\,MHz word clock, then CML 8:4, 4:2 and the half-rate 2:1; the RX adds one CML 1:2 in front of the CMOS deserializer.
  \item \textbf{DFE tap 1 is unrolled.} The direct tap-1 loop needs about 140\,ps (\cref{sec:dfe-timing}), more than the 100\,ps UI, so tap 1 becomes speculative, the fallback already listed in \cref{tab:specs}. Each data phase gets two samplers.
  \item \textbf{Pad and package.} For 10\,dB of return loss at 5\,GHz the pad may carry only half the capacitance, 0.42\,pF, and a bond wire only half the inductance, about 0.5\,mm (\cref{we:ch14-bond}). The T-coil of \cref{sec:esd-pad} stops being a fallback and becomes part of the pad.
  \item \textbf{Power.} CML draws its tails whatever the rate, so only the additions cost power: the extra CML stages, faster clock buffers, two more samplers and the dividers add about 65\,mW. That gives $\approx$280\,mW, or about \textbf{28\,pJ/b} at 10\,Gb/s against 43\,pJ/b at 5\,Gb/s.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}{Does the timing budget close at 10\,Gb/s?}\label{we:ch14-10g}
\textbf{Given:} the 5\,Gb/s budget of \cref{sec:jitter-budget}: TX DCD/DDJ 8\,ps, residual ISI 6\,ps, RX clock path 6\,ps, CDR dither 20\,ps; RJ $\sigma$ = 1.32\,ps (RSS of 1.0, 0.7 and 0.5\,ps); $2Q$ = 14.07 at $10^{-12}$; target TJ $\le0.3$\,UI.

\textbf{Same circuits at 10\,Gb/s:} residual ISI and CDR dither scale with the UI (3 and 10\,ps); duty-cycle error, clock-path jitter and RJ are set in picoseconds and stay. DJ = 8 + 3 + 6 + 10 = 27\,ps; $2Q\sigma$ = 18.6\,ps; TJ = 45.6\,ps = 0.46\,UI. The budget fails.

\textbf{Targets:} duty-cycle correction to DCD + DDJ $\le$ 4\,ps, clock path $\le$ 3\,ps, CDR dither $\le$ 8\,ps give DJ = 18\,ps. Then $2Q\sigma \le 30 - 18 = 12$\,ps, so $\sigma\le$ 0.85\,ps rms, for example 0.5\,ps from the PLL, 0.5\,ps from the clock path and 0.45\,ps from the TX (RSS 0.84\,ps).

\textbf{Result:} at 10\,Gb/s every jitter source specified in picoseconds must roughly halve. That is why the PLL row of \cref{tab:specs} asks for 0.5\,ps rms at 10\,GHz, and why \cref{fig:ch14-rate}(b) shows timing, not the eye height, as the 10\,Gb/s problem.
\end{example}

\begin{insight}[Why not 25\,Gb/s?]
SiGe BiCMOS of this class has been used for clock recovery at far higher rates. But past about 12\,Gb/s over these channels the architecture changes: a quarter-rate or all-CML datapath with eight calibrated clock phases, more DFE taps with adaptation, clocks below 300\,fs rms, flip-chip or a better package, and channels under about 25\,cm. That is a different chip and a larger team, so it belongs to Phase~4 of \cref{sec:ch14-roadmap}.
\end{insight}

\section{From transceiver to test chip}\label{sec:ch14-testchip}

\Cref{fig:arch} is the transceiver. To measure it, the transceiver must sit on a die with test logic around it, a floorplan that respects its three boundaries, and a package that does not ruin its 2.5\,GHz signals. This section shows the concept for each. The details will change as the blocks are designed, but the rules behind them will not.

\subsection{The test chip}

\Cref{fig:ch14-testchip} is the chip-level view of \cref{fig:arch}, with the test logic made explicit. Digital blocks sit on the left: SPI configuration registers that reach every block, a PRBS7/31 generator for the TX, and a PRBS checker with an error counter for the RX. The TX row runs across the top (serializer, 3-tap FFE, CML driver), and the RX row runs back along the bottom (CTLE, sampler with DFE, deserializer). The LC-PLL takes an external reference and clocks the TX; the CDR steers the sampling phase from the data itself. Two paths close the loop. The internal loopback sends the driver output straight to the RX input, which tests the whole chip with no board at all. The external path goes through a test PCB, SMA cables and trace coupons, which adds a real channel.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]

The error counter is what makes a cheap lab possible. With PRBS31 running and the counter at zero, a BER below $10^{-12}$ is shown at 95\,\% confidence after $3/\mathrm{BER} = 3\times10^{12}$ bits, which takes 10 minutes at 5\,Gb/s (\cref{sec:labtest}). An oscilloscope then only has to show the eye, not count errors.

\subsection{Floorplan}

\Cref{fig:ch14-floorplan} is a first floorplan. Each placement follows from a number in an earlier chapter:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TX and RX pads on one edge,} so the high-speed bond wires are the shortest on the die (\cref{we:ch14-bond}).
  \item \textbf{The LC-PLL in its own corner,} with a guard ring and its own supply, away from the switching digital core. The 4\,nH inductor needs keep-out space on TopMetal2 (\cref{sec:layout-hs}).
  \item \textbf{TX and RX next to their pads, with the CDR between them,} because the I/Q clocks and the PI outputs must reach four samplers with matched delays (\cref{sec:quadrature}).
  \item \textbf{Three supply domains:} AVDD at about 2.5\,V for the HBT CML, DVDD at 1.2\,V for CMOS, and VDDIO at 3.3\,V for the pads. This is the supply boundary of \cref{sec:arch-block}, drawn on silicon.
  \item \textbf{A digital core hardened with LibreLane,} and the seal ring, fill and logo taken from the JKU template flow~\cite{jkutemplate}.
\end{itemize}
The die is about $2\times2$\,mm$^2$ for now. The PLL alone is 0.347\,mm$^2$~\cite{smacd2026pll}, and the final size waits for the block layouts.

\begin{example}{How long may a high-speed bond wire be?}\label{we:ch14-bond}
\textbf{Given:} a bond wire of $L \approx 1$\,nH per mm in series between two \qty{50}{\ohm} environments; a return-loss goal of 10\,dB at $\fN = 2.5$\,GHz, the same goal as for the pad in \cref{tab:specs}.

\textbf{Return loss of a series inductor:} with $x = \omega L/(2\Zo)$, $\lvert S_{11}\rvert = x/\sqrt{1+x^2}$ (\cref{we:ch3-bond}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item 1\,mm, 1\,nH: $x = 2\pi\cdot2.5\,\mathrm{GHz}\cdot1\,\mathrm{nH}/100\,\Omega = 0.157$, so $\lvert S_{11}\rvert = 0.155$ and RL = 16.2\,dB.
  \item 2.5\,mm, 2.5\,nH: $x = 0.393$, so RL = 8.7\,dB. This already misses the goal, before the pad capacitance is added.
  \item 3\,mm, 3\,nH (a corner pad of a large QFN): RL = 7.4\,dB.
  \item At 5\,GHz, the Nyquist frequency of the 10\,Gb/s target (\cref{sec:ch14-10g}), 1\,mm gives $x = 0.314$ and RL = 10.5\,dB, with nothing left for the pad; 0.5\,mm gives 16.2\,dB again.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Result:} keep the high-speed bond wires at or below about 1\,mm, and about 0.5\,mm for the 10\,Gb/s target. That rule puts the TX and RX pads on the die edge closest to their package pins, and puts those pins in the middle of one package side. If the wires cannot be that short, part of the inductance can be absorbed into the T-coil of \cref{sec:esd-pad}.
\end{example}

\section{Power budget}\label{sec:power-budget}

A CML gate draws its tail current whatever the data, so a CML-heavy chip has a power budget you can write down before any layout: list the tails, multiply by the supply. CMOS logic burns $\alpha C V^2 f$ instead.

\begin{keyeq}[CML power and energy per bit]
\begin{equation}
  P_\mathrm{CML} = V_\mathrm{CC}\sum_i I_{T,i},\qquad P_\mathrm{CMOS}=\alpha\,C_\mathrm{sw}V_\mathrm{DD}^2f,\qquad E_b = \frac{P_\mathrm{total}}{\fb}
  \label{eq:ch14-eb}
\end{equation}
\end{keyeq}

\Cref{tab:ch14-power} collects the estimates of \cref{ch:tx,ch:rx,ch:pll,ch:cdr} and \cref{fig:ch14-power} plots them. Assumptions: 1\,Vppd swing (20\,mA driver tail); CML gates as in \cref{we:ch9-hbt}, about 1.2\,mA each with emitter followers; CML on 2.5\,V, CMOS on 1.2\,V; the team's measured PLL power of 12.73\,mW~\cite{smacd2026pll}. The polyphase buffers, the PIs and the sampler clock buffers are separate circuits and are counted separately.

\begin{example}{Energy per bit, and where to save}\label{we:ch14-power}
\textbf{Total:} the CML tails add up to 77\,mA from 2.5\,V (192\,mW); the PLL, the CMOS blocks and the DACs add 25\,mW: $P\approx\qty{216}{\milli\watt}$. By \cref{eq:ch14-eb}, $E_b = 216\,\mathrm{mW}/5\,\mathrm{Gb/s} = \mathbf{43.2}$\,pJ/b.

\textbf{Comparison:} the Purdue OpenSerDes spends $438\,\mathrm{mW}/2\,\mathrm{Gb/s} = 219$\,pJ/b~\cite{openserdes2021}. \OS{} runs 2.5 times faster for about half the power, five times less energy per bit, because the HBTs do in a few CML gates what an all-digital 130\,nm CMOS design must do with oversampling and many more gates.

\textbf{Savings, largest first:} (1) the SST driver of \cref{sec:cml-driver} saves about 44\,mW, giving 172\,mW and 34.4\,pJ/b; (2) a swing of 0.8\,Vppd saves 10\,mW; (3) each CML gate removed from the clock paths saves about 3\,mW. Lowering the CML supply would save the most of all, but every $V_\mathrm{CE}$ must still fit below $\mathrm{BV}_\mathrm{CEO}$ with the swing on top (\cref{sec:cml-driver}), so this waits for silicon data.
\end{example}

\section{Risks and open questions}\label{sec:ch14-risks}

The architecture closes on paper. It can still fail in these places, roughly in order of the cost of a late fix:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ESD and pad capacitance.} ESD diodes can take most of the 0.85\,pF budget alone; get the \sgx{} ESD cell capacitance early, and keep the T-coil of \cref{sec:esd-pad} as the fallback.
  \item \textbf{$\mathrm{BV}_\mathrm{CEO}$ headroom.} The off driver transistor sits at 1.25--1.5\,V against about 1.6\,V; simulate every $V_\mathrm{CE}$ at every corner with the real termination.
  \item \textbf{PLL phase noise.} The quoted $-100.8$\,dBc/Hz at 1\,MHz is far worse than Leeson predicts (\cref{sec:lcvco}); the 1\,ps RJ line depends on what it really is.
  \item \textbf{Inductor EM modeling.} The 4\,nH tank and any T-coil must be EM-simulated on TopMetal2 (\cref{sec:layout-hs}) and matched to the team's measured $Q$.
  \item \textbf{Timing loops.} DFE tap 1 uses about 140 of its 200\,ps and the CDR dither 19 of its 20\,ps; both need post-layout confirmation.
  \item \textbf{The 10\,GHz clock and the 10\,Gb/s timing budget.} The VCO is a new design, and the budget needs every picosecond term roughly halved (\cref{we:ch14-10g}). Put the VCO and its $\div2$ on the Phase-1 shuttle, and give the test chip an external clock input so that both modes can be measured without the on-chip PLL.
  \item \textbf{Packaging and measurement.} Bond wires longer than about 1\,mm (0.5\,mm at 10\,Gb/s) cost return loss (\cref{we:ch14-bond}); the test PCB, a clean reference clock and access to a sampling scope or BERT must be ready before first silicon.
  \item \textbf{Area, pads and the channel.} The PLL is 0.347\,mm$^2$~\cite{smacd2026pll}, the MIM capacitors add tenths of a mm$^2$, and the high-speed pads need SMA-grade access; REF must be replaced by measured S-parameters (\cref{sec:ch16-next}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{takeaways}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \OS{} delivers six things: the GDSII, the RTL and benches, the link model and GUI, an HBT/CML cell library, documentation, and a paper with open data.
  \item \OS{} is a single-lane transceiver: CMOS at 312.5\,MHz in the core, HBT CML at 1.25\,GHz and above, joined by level converters, two supplies and two clock domains.
  \item One 2.5\,GHz LC PLL feeds both sides: the TX divides it; the RX makes four phases and rotates them in 6.25\,ps steps under digital CDR control.
  \item The block specification table is the contract: every block has a 5\,Gb/s and a 10\,Gb/s number and a chapter; $^\dagger$ marks estimates awaiting simulation.
  \item Design for 10, guarantee 5: the same architecture carries 10\,Gb/s NRZ over 0.5\,m of FR-4 (193\,mV worst-case eye) because the equalizer only sees the loss at Nyquist. The work is in the clocks and the timing budget (RJ $\le0.85$\,ps rms), not in the transistors.
  \item The voltage budget leaves about 76\,mV; the timing budget leaves a 141\,ps eye with 1.4\,ps to spare.
  \item The test chip adds SPI, a PRBS generator and checker with an error counter, and an internal loopback; TX and RX pads share one die edge, and high-speed bond wires stay at or below about 1\,mm.
  \item Five phases, each with an exit criterion and a tagged release: feasibility, building blocks, the 5/10\,Gb/s transceiver, measurement, and beyond.
  \item First-cut power is about 216\,mW (43\,pJ/b), five times less energy per bit than OpenSerDes, dominated by static CML current; the SST driver is the first saving.
\end{itemize}
\end{takeaways}
